HOW WE GOT TO THE VALUES · SCORING · WEEK 9, 2026

How your scoring reaches DDF Value, and what we tested to make it better

DDF Value is the average of four trade charts after each is turned into value above your league's waiver line. Two of the four keep the same order within each position whatever your scoring. We tried to fix that ourselves. This page shows how DDF Value handles scoring today, what we built, and what our tests found.

In short

  • CBS Sports and USA Today publish a column for each scoring format. We use the one that matches your league.
  • FantasyPros publishes one Value column for every format. FantasyCalc publishes its numbers at a 12-team Full PPR setting; its other scoring settings change values slightly by multiplying each position by one number, so no two backs ever change places.
  • We built a way to rescore those two charts from projected stats, and tested it against points players actually scored.
  • It did not clearly beat today's DDF Value. The changes were small and inside the noise of four weeks of games. We are not switching to it yet.

How we know FantasyCalc's base is 12-team Full PPR: in its public data, each player's value equals its redraft value only at that setting, for all 196 players. Every other scoring and team-count setting is that list times one number per position, to within rounding. Its 2-QB setting is a separate list. What we cannot see is which leagues' trades feed that list, so treating it as a Full PPR list is an assumption.

What DDF Value does with scoring today

  1. Each chart's list at your scoring. CBS Sports and USA Today change. FantasyPros does not. FantasyCalc changes only by one number per position, so its order within a position stays the same.
  2. Projected points at your scoring. Three projection sets (ESPN, CBS Sports rest of season, Razzball) project points per game in each format. Those points decide how many players at each position start and sit on benches in your league, and so where each position's waiver line falls.
  3. The split of value between positions. It averages the four charts and the three projection sets, so the projections move it with your scoring.

So a Full PPR league already gives receivers more of the value than a Standard league does, through steps 2 and 3. What no step does is reorder two backs inside FantasyPros or FantasyCalc when one catches 60 passes and the other does not.

What we built

  1. Give each chart a scoring. We first tried to read a chart's scoring from its order and spacing. Tested on CBS Sports and USA Today, where the answer is known, it picked the right format in 3 of 20 cases, so we dropped it. We use what the publisher shows instead, and say so: FantasyCalc at the 12-team Full PPR setting its numbers are published at (assumed, since its trade mix is not public), and FantasyPros, which states nothing, at Half PPR, the guess with the smallest worst-case error.
  2. Turn the chart into points. For each chart and position we fit its values against projected rest-of-season points at that scoring. That keeps the chart's own order and spacing and puts it in points.
  3. Split points into games and stats. Projected games left and each player's projected stat line per game (passing, rushing, receiving, receptions, turnovers) say how his points would change under another scoring. The chart supplies only the total; the games and the stat mix come from the projections.
  4. Rescore and convert back. Each player's points move by his projected change, for example a point per catch from Standard to Full PPR. Then the chart's values go through the same value-above-waivers steps as today.

On DDF Value the change would be labelled: "Includes 2 charts adjusted to your scoring (FantasyCalc, FantasyPros)." Each adjusted column would say the publisher did not make it.

What it would change

In a 12-team Standard league, week 9: James Cook 66.7 to 71.1, Derrick Henry 39.2 to 43.7; Trey McBride 26.1 to 20.4, Puka Nacua 51.9 to 47.7, Chase Brown 31.8 to 28.1. Running backs' share of all value goes from 55.8% to 56.5%. In Half PPR the moves are about a point or two; in Full PPR, under a point, because only FantasyPros changes there.

The rescoring reorders more than the publishers do. Between our rescored Standard and Full PPR lists it swaps 65 of those 1,260 pairs on FantasyCalc and 68 on FantasyPros, where CBS Sports' own Standard and Full columns differ by 4 and USA Today's by 21. Those counts leave out pairs a column ties (CBS Sports 68, USA Today 41; our rescored lists almost none); counting each tie as half a swap still gives 38 and about 42.

Did it work?

We wrote the tests and what would count as failing before building the rescoring, and logged every later change to them before running any test. The yardstick is points players actually scored at each scoring, which no chart and no part of DDF controls: 2026 weeks 5 to 8, measured from each week's values. That is four short windows, so small differences cannot be told apart from luck.

Actual points are not trade value. These tests say which values track what players score at each scoring. They cannot say what a trade partner would accept, so they do not test whether FantasyCalc's flat response reflects how people really trade.

Within each position, projected points alone were ahead of DDF Value and FantasyCalc on actual points, though not by a margin four weeks can confirm; FantasyPros as published matched them in Half PPR and edged them in Full PPR. FantasyPros alone, unadjusted, was also ahead of DDF Value within each position at every scoring (0.365 vs 0.325 in Standard), a comparison we did not plan in advance and have no range for: which charts go into the average may matter more than how each handles scoring. On trades across positions projections trailed, and moved ahead only after we converted them to value above the waiver line, a version we had not written into the tests, and still not significantly. Over one to four weeks that is what projections are built for; it does not make them better trade values.

Two of the failures teach something. Rescoring the publishers' own Standard columns toward their own Full columns matched best at only about a third of full strength (0.3 on CBS Sports, 0.4 on USA Today); that test looks at order within a position, so it does not measure how far their values move, only which strength of rescoring best fits their rankings. And over eleven seasons, a player's past Full PPR points, catches included, predicted his future Standard points better than his past Standard points did: catches mean he gets the ball, and that predicts yards and touchdowns too. Rescoring by catches at full strength ignores both.

What happens next

DDF Value stays as it is. FantasyPros and FantasyCalc are still averaged at their published numbers.

Two versions are worth testing next, each on weeks we have not looked at yet, with its tests written first: a rescoring at about a third of full strength, the strength that best matched the publishers' own format columns; and rescoring FantasyPros while leaving FantasyCalc out of the average, the one variant whose trade result cleared zero (Standard, +0.006 [+0.001, +0.011]), which we found after the fact and so do not count.
