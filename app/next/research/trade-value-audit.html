Research · Week 9, 2026

They're all wrong, some more than others

Every fantasy trade argument ends the same way: somebody posts a chart. But a trade chart is a model with opinions baked in. How much is a running back worth next to a receiver? What changes in superflex? Do two quarters make a dollar? We put four widely available redraft trade charts through the same tests on the same week. None of them gets everything right. Some miss by a lot more than others.

Snapshot: analyst charts published 2026-11-03 (saved by 2026-11-04), FantasyCalc pulled 2026-11-07 · tests run on a 12-team, Half PPR league unless noted · source pages read 2026-11-07

What DDF Value is

DDF Value (Data Driven Football Value) is the value we built and the reference line for every comparison on this page. We start from the four redraft charts graded here: CBS Sports, USA Today, FantasyPros and FantasyCalc. Each chart's numbers are converted to value above the waiver line. That line is replacement level: the value of the best player still available for free once every team has filled its starters and bench, so a player is worth only what he adds above it; then each is adjusted for your league's settings (scoring, team count, lineup) and the four are averaged. DDF Value also weights starters and bench differently: each player's value above the waiver line is split by how often he is expected to start (from missed games, byes and your lineup), and the starting part is priced at a higher rate than the bench part, using separate starter and bench budgets. The split of value between positions is set by averaging seven sources' position mixes: the four charts plus three projection sets our model uses as inputs, so three sevenths of that split comes from projections.

The research behind it. The waiver line comes from how many players your league rosters at each position. The starter and bench weighting comes from measured rates, not assumptions: how often a healthy starter misses his team's games, measured on NFL weekly results from 2015 to 2025 (running backs 15.3%, receivers 10.5%), together with bye weeks and the spread between projections. The method is written up step by step in our methodology, and the tests on this page are how we check each chart against the result.

Because DDF Value is built from the same charts, measuring a chart against it shows how far that chart sits from a league-aware consensus of all four, not how far it sits from an independent truth.

The strongest objections, answered

  • "You grade the charts against your own average of them." Partly true, and the page is built around it. The distance columns measure how far a chart sits from the league-aware consensus of all four, not error. Three findings do not depend on DDF Value's numbers: a scoring change should reorder players, two players are worth only what each adds above a free pickup (the waiver line comes from roster counts), and with one tight end starting per team the best one should be worth well short of the best player. Those rest on the scoring and lineup rules.
  • "A consensus of the same charts adds no information." Averaging alone would not. What DDF Value adds is the league layer the charts leave out: value above your league's waiver line, a lower weight for bench value than starting value, and an adjustment to your scoring, team count and lineup. It cannot know more about a player than the four charts do.
  • "Your position split is not just the charts." Correct. Today the split averages seven sources' position mixes, the four charts and three projection sets, so three sevenths of it comes from projections. A charts-only split is built and switched off; the live setting averages all seven. That helps explain the two largest gaps on the page: running backs in Standard (55.7% for DDF Value against 51.1% for the Avg of Analyst charts) and superflex quarterbacks (34.9% against a four-chart average of 27.3%).
  • "Your 34.9% superflex quarterback share is a guess." It is a modeling judgment. The direction is settled by the lineup rules: about 24 quarterbacks start each week, and the first one past a superflex league's roster projects 0.7 points per game. The size is settled only by real superflex trade prices, which this article does not measure.
  • "Your injury margin is too small." The starter and bench weights rest on measured missed-game rates (running backs 15.3%, receivers 10.5%). A chart that builds in a bigger margin is making a different judgment, not a mistake.
  • "The charts are priced for different jobs." They are. CBS Sports and USA Today publish analyst charts for the rest of the season, FantasyPros combines its analysts' rankings, and FantasyCalc is a live market of trades. None states a league size, so every test assumes 12 teams with our lineup; a deeper or shallower league moves the waiver line and the depth and 2-for-1 results with it.
  • "They agree on order, so the differences don't matter." Their rank correlations run 0.831 to 0.925, so they mostly agree on who is better. They disagree on price, and price is what decides a trade.
  • "This is one week." It is: the analyst charts were published 2026-11-03 and FantasyCalc was pulled 2026-11-07, four days later; USA Today's capture carries an amber data-check flag; and the grade thresholds are ours, written under the scorecard so anyone can re-cut them.

In short

Before the verdicts: the strongest objections to our method, answered.

CBS Sports and FantasyCalc hold up better. Their curves sit closest to DDF Value's: CBS Sports sits 2.8 and FantasyCalc 3.4 percentage points from DDF Value's curve on average, so adding two mid-tier players rarely buys a star (2% and 8% of comparisons after subtracting replacement value). CBS Sports also prints a column for each scoring format. Their misses: CBS Sports lists too few players to reach the end of a 12-team bench and gives superflex quarterbacks the smallest share of the four (22.1%, Far from DDF Value), and FantasyCalc's scoring switch rescales whole positions without reordering anyone; FantasyCalc also lists well past the waiver line at quarterback and prices its top tight end at 63% of its best player.

FantasyPros and USA Today are weaker where it matters most in a trade. Their curves are flat (the 24th player is still worth 51% and 65% of the #1, against 38% and 46% on the other two), so two mid-tier players beat a top-12 star in 45% and 42% of comparisons even after subtracting replacement value. FantasyPros uses one number for every scoring format; USA Today prices its top tight end at 80% of its best player and lists far past the waiver line.

Which trades to lean on each chart for

Tendencies from one snapshot, not trade instructions. "Be careful" means the chart sits far from the league-aware consensus or breaks a first-principles rule (a scoring change should reorder players; replacement value matters), not that it is proven wrong.

CBS Sports

  • Lean on it for: 2-for-1 and star-for-depth deals (steepest curve through the top 24; 2% of pairs beat a star after replacement value) and Standard or Full PPR trades (a column per format).
  • Be careful with: deep bench-for-bench deals (it lists 43 running backs, so 22 of the 65 a 12-team league rosters get no number) and superflex quarterback deals (22.1% share against DDF Value's 34.9%: quarterbacks look cheap, which flatters the buyer, not the seller).

FantasyCalc

  • Lean on it for: 2-for-1 deals (8% after replacement value), trades in 10- or 14-team leagues (it takes league size as a setting) and superflex (2QB values for every quarterback it lists).
  • Be careful with: back-for-back or back-for-pass-catcher trades where PPR matters (its switch rescales positions and never reorders within one) and elite tight end asks (63% of its #1 against DDF Value's 42%).

FantasyPros

  • Lean on it for: superflex quarterback prices (34.6%, 0.3 points from DDF Value) and tight end prices (37% of its #1).
  • Be careful with: anything where scoring format matters (one Value column for every format) and 2-for-1s on raw sums (68% of pairs beat a star raw, 45% after replacement value).

USA Today

  • Lean on it for: trades where scoring format matters (its columns reorder 59% of the top 36, the most of the four) and superflex coverage (38 quarterbacks).
  • Be careful with: 2-for-1s (42% of pairs beat a star even after replacement value), selling an elite tight end (80% of its #1 against DDF Value's 42%) and deep-bench prices (it lists far past the waiver line). Its capture this week carries an amber data-check flag.

None of them gets everything right, so we built our own. DDF Value keeps what each chart knows about players and corrects what a single chart cannot: it prices value above your league's waiver line and counts a bench player's value at a lower weight than a starter's, so sums of players mean something, and it adjusts every chart to your scoring, team count and lineup. It is a consensus of the same charts, so it is a better-informed starting point, not a proof that the others are wrong.

How we graded

We used the numbers each chart published for week 9, as our pipeline saved them, and asked nine questions of each. Most tests use only the chart's own numbers: does a number change when the scoring changes, how many players does the chart list, how often do two lesser players add up to a star. Where a test needs a reference, it uses DDF Value, defined above. DDF Value is the reference line, not a contestant, so it has no grades. Because it is built from the same charts, these tests measure how each chart differs from that league-aware consensus, not from an independent truth; where a finding rests on first principles instead, we say so.

The scale is simple: Strong, OK, Weak, or Not measured when we lack the data or the test does not apply. The exact rule for each grade sits under the table. A grade describes one snapshot, not a chart's whole season.

1. Position balance

Of all the value in a league, how much goes to each position? We measured each chart's share of value above waivers by position and compared it with DDF Value's. The gap is the share of value that would have to move between positions to match DDF Value, in percentage points: half the total of the four position differences. For example, a chart with 5 points more at WR and 5 fewer at RB has a gap of 5. In a Half PPR, one-QB league every chart is within about 6 percentage points, so the four mostly agree on how a league's value splits (FantasyPros 4, FantasyCalc 5, CBS Sports 5.2, USA Today 5.9). CBS Sports, FantasyCalc and USA Today lean toward receivers (5.2, 3.9 and 3.6 points); FantasyPros leans toward running backs (4).

Press the scoring buttons for the bigger pattern. The Avg of Analyst charts (the average of CBS Sports, USA Today and FantasyPros) give running backs 51.1% of the value in Standard and 41.1% in Full PPR. DDF Value gives them 55.7% and 41.3%; its larger Standard figure comes partly from the projection inputs in its position budget (see How we tested), so read the Standard gap as a difference in method, not as an error in the charts.

This test measures each chart's numbers inside one league structure: how many backs and receivers start in each format is the reference's allocation, so a chart's share moves with scoring even when its numbers do not (FantasyPros' running backs go from 58.4% in Standard to 45.9% in Full PPR on identical numbers). It compares charts with each other and with DDF Value in that same structure.

On running backs. Backs miss more games, and DDF Value's league adjustment already counts on it: it expects running backs to miss 15.3% of games and receivers 10.5%. A chart that wants a bigger safety margin than that is making a judgment, not an error.

2. The shape of the curve: how fast does value fall?

Line up every player from best to worst and divide by the #1 player. A steep curve says stars are rare and expensive. A flat one says depth is nearly as good. By the 24th player, CBS Sports has fallen to 38% of its #1, DDF Value to 40%, FantasyCalc to 46%, FantasyPros to 51% and USA Today only to 65%. Toggle the scoring and watch which lines bend. One of them never does.

3. Does the chart know your scoring?

Switch from Standard to Full PPR and the order of players should change: a running back who catches 60 passes should pass one who does not. That follows from the scoring rules alone. We took the top 36 running backs, receivers and tight ends on each list and counted how many change places. USA Today moves 59% and CBS Sports 32%, both from separate published columns per format; DDF Value, the reference, moves 37%. FantasyCalc changes every number but none of its top 99 change places within their position: running backs and receivers each get one multiplier, and tight ends and quarterbacks vary more at the bottom of their lists without reordering the top. FantasyPros changes nothing.

How we checked that this is them, not us. FantasyCalc: our pipeline makes one request per format to its public values endpoint (ppr=0 for Standard, ppr=1 for Full PPR, 12 teams, one QB) and stores the published number untouched; the multiplier is in those raw responses, not in any adjustment of ours. We repeated the two requests live on 2026-11-07 and got the same pattern. In 8 captures from 2026-09-29 to 2026-11-07, only 1, the oldest (2026-09-29), reordered players (10 of the top 36 running backs), so this looks like a change on FantasyCalc's side around the start of October. Independently reproduced by a second model: two separate checks on 2026-11-07, each fetching fresh with the same public parameters (ppr=0, 0.5 and 1; numQbs=1; numTeams=12) and re-reading the FantasyPros article without seeing our results, found no top running back, receiver or tight end changing places on FantasyCalc, multipliers of about 0.994 and 1.018 on its rounded published numbers, and no scoring variants on FantasyPros to compare. FantasyPros: the week 9 article's four tables carry one Value column (plus 2QB and tight end premium columns) and no scoring selector (read 2026-11-07), and its numbers were identical across formats in every weekly snapshot from week 5 to week 9.

A fair note for CBS Sports and USA Today: their quarterback numbers are the same in every format because neither publishes a quarterback column per scoring format. We use CBS Sports' one-QB 4-point column and USA Today's one-QB column; neither chart's 6-point passing touchdown column is used here.

4. Superflex: the quarterback discount

All four redraft charts publish superflex or 2QB numbers for 32 to 38 quarterbacks, enough for a 12-team league. Credit where due. Move to superflex and all four lift quarterbacks from about 8.3% of all value to between 22.1% (CBS Sports) and 34.6% (FantasyPros), an average of 27.3%. A superflex league puts about 24 quarterbacks in lineups each week out of 32 NFL starters, so the direction of the move follows from the lineup rules; the charts disagree on its size by 12.5 points. DDF Value gives quarterbacks 34.9%; its position budget includes our model's projection inputs (see How we tested), which is why it sits above the charts' average. The scorecard keeps the two apart: Superflex coverage asks whether a chart publishes superflex values for at least 24 quarterbacks (all four do), and QB share in superflex vs DDF Value measures how far its share sits from DDF Value's (CBS Sports 12.8 points, Far; USA Today 8.9 and FantasyCalc 8.4, OK; FantasyPros 0.3, Close). This test measures each chart's quarterback share; it does not measure what managers pay in real superflex trades.

5. Depth: does the chart reach the end of your bench?

Every position has a waiver line: the value of the best player you could pick up for free once every team has filled its starters and bench. League size and roster slots set it. With our default lineup, a 12-team league rosters about 20 quarterbacks, 65 running backs, 62 receivers and 21 tight ends, so the line sits at the next player at each position (10 teams: 18, 59, 48, 15; 14 teams: 24, 74, 73, 25). Value above that line is value above waivers, the basis of DDF Value (how DDF Value is built).

A chart's list should end at that line. None of the four states a roster size, so each is measured against a 12-team league's line. Stopping short leaves real roster value unpriced; reaching past puts a price on players the waiver wire can replace. Both miss the target, and the depth grade counts them the same way: the gap between players listed and players rostered at each position, as a share of players rostered, averaged over the four positions. Here is where each list ends in a 12-team league.

  • CBS Sports stops short at every position: 13 QBs, 43 RBs, 43 WRs and 11 TEs. Its last running back, Devin Singletary, is still worth 8.8% of its top back, and 22 rostered backs get no number at all. CBS Sports covers them with a rule (any unlisted player is worth no more than four points), so in a bench-for-bench trade it cannot tell your 50th back from your 60th.
  • USA Today reaches past the line at every position. 5.3% of all its value goes to players beyond it, the most of any chart, and its waiver-level tight end is still worth 11.1% of its top tight end. That is value placed on players the waiver wire can replace.
  • FantasyPros is mixed: past the line at QB and WR, short at RB (52 of 65), near it at TE. Its receiver at the line is still worth 14.5% of its top receiver, so it places real value on receivers at the waiver line.
  • FantasyCalc puts the least value past the line of the charts that reach past it: near the line at RB, and past it at QB, WR and TE only with players worth almost nothing on its scale; just 0.6% of its value sits past the line.

In value, the misses are small next to the counts. In a 12-team league CBS Sports gives no number to 35% of rostered players, but they hold 2.7% of the pie by DDF Value, because the players it skips sit near the line. On the other side, USA Today puts 5.3% of its own value past the line, FantasyPros 2% and FantasyCalc 0.6%. The depth grade counts players, so it shows where a list ends; these shares show how much value is at stake.

6. The 2-for-1 test

Here is where curve shape stops being academic. Add up two chart numbers and compare them with one, and you have priced a 2-for-1. But the team that takes two players needs a roster spot, and a freely available player at each position has some value on most charts. Subtract each player's replacement value (the waiver-level value at his position) and the math changes; this does not model the specific player your team would cut. The test uses only this subtraction; full DDF Value also counts bench value at a lower weight than starting value. Across every comparison of a pair ranked 25 to 60 with a top-12 player (630 pairs times 12 stars, all weighted equally), on FantasyPros 68% favor the pair on raw numbers and 45% after the subtraction. On USA Today, 61% and 42%. CBS Sports and FantasyCalc are steep enough that the subtraction barely matters. CBS Sports also states a rule for lopsided deals: take 10% off when one side gets at least two more players [1].

Try one yourself. The default is a sample week 9 comparison on raw chart numbers; tick the box to subtract each player's replacement value. The box works on each chart's numbers as a share of its own #1 player, which keeps every chart's ratios.

7. Where the charts disagree

The good news: the charts mostly agree on order. Across the 109 players all four list, rank correlations run from 0.831 to 0.925. The fights are about price, and about a handful of players. Pick a chart to see where it parts from the other three. Points above the dashed line are players this chart ranks higher than the other three do; points below, lower.

8. Weekly change

Our saved weekly snapshots differ for every source from week 5 to week 9, and every one of them moved its top players. The analyst charts keep about a quarter to a half of their numbers exactly the same from one week to the next. FantasyCalc changed every one of those numbers every week. This test measures week-to-week change in what we saved; it does not measure how fast a source reacts to news.

What we would expect, and why

For each finding: what we would expect from first principles, what the charts show, where DDF Value lands, and how to check it. DDF Value is shown for reference; agreeing with it does not make a chart right.

1. FantasyPros and scoring formats

Expect: players change places between Standard and Full PPR (first principles); the two charts with a column per format move 32% to 59% of their top 36, DDF Value 37%. Shows: 0%. Gap: no movement at all.

Why: a catch is worth a point in Full PPR and nothing in Standard, and players catch very different numbers of passes, so the order has to move. This is a first principle, independent of DDF Value. Check it on the CBS Sports chart, whose NON and PPR columns order players differently. For your trade: in Standard or Full PPR, use a chart with a column for your format.

2. FantasyCalc and scoring formats

Expect: the same movement within each position (first principles). Shows: 0 of 99 reorder; every top back gets about 0.994 (spread 0.001). Gap: the order within a position never moves.

Why: one factor per position can shift backs against receivers, but it cannot lift a pass-catching back over a runner; only player-level scoring can. That is arithmetic, independent of DDF Value. Check it with the two FantasyCalc links in the headline. For your trade: FantasyCalc's format switch changes how positions trade against each other, not how two backs compare.

3. The 2-for-1 test

Expect: few pairs of mid-tier players worth more than a top-12 star once each player's replacement value is subtracted (first principles). DDF Value has: 9%. Shows: after the subtraction, FantasyPros 45%, USA Today 42%, FantasyCalc 8%, CBS Sports 2%. Gap: 36 and 33 points above the reference for FantasyPros and USA Today.

Why: a lineup starts only so many players, so two players are worth what each adds above a free pickup, and a player who mostly sits on the bench adds less than one who starts. The chart figures here apply only the first part; DDF Value's applies both. The first-principles part, subtracting replacement value, does not depend on DDF Value; the size of the gap does. Check it on the FantasyPros chart with any two players ranked around 30th. For your trade: if you accept replacement-level math, a 2-for-1 that looks fair on a flat chart favors the side getting the star.

4. Quarterbacks in superflex

Expect: a large lift from one-QB to superflex (first principles: about 24 quarterbacks start each week out of 32). Shows: every chart lifts them, from about 8.3% to 22.1% to 34.6%. Our view: a large share, because the replacement quarterback in superflex is a backup who scores few or no points (0.7 projected points per game this week); our model puts it at roughly a third, a modeling judgment. DDF Value has 34.9%, with projection inputs in its position budget. Gap: the charts differ from each other by 12.5 points.

Why: the direction is settled by the lineup rules; the size is a judgment each chart makes, and real superflex trade prices are the test that would settle it. Check the size on the CBS Sports chart, which prints 1QB and 2QB columns side by side. For your trade: in superflex, quarterback prices vary most between charts; check two before you quote one.

5. The top tight end

Expect: the top tight end worth well short of the #1 player (first principles: one tight end starts per team and the position falls off quickly). DDF Value has: 42%. Shows: USA Today 80%, FantasyCalc 63%, CBS Sports 42%, FantasyPros 37%. Gap: USA Today 38 and FantasyCalc 21 points above; CBS Sports level; FantasyPros -5.

Why: a league starts one tight end per team and the position falls off quickly after the top few, so the best tight end is worth a lot but well short of a top running back; that shape is a first principle. DDF Value's figure comes from the league-adjusted charts, with projection inputs in its position budget. Check it on the USA Today charts and the CBS Sports chart. For your trade: on USA Today an elite tight end prices close to a top player; the other charts put him well below.

What this means for your trade

  1. In a 2-for-1, subtract each player's replacement value. On FantasyPros and USA Today, adding two numbers makes depth look rich. CBS Sports and FantasyCalc are steep enough that it matters less.
  2. Check the scoring before you quote a chart. FantasyPros has one number for every format, and none of FantasyCalc's top 99 running backs, receivers and tight ends change places within their position between Standard and Full PPR. CBS Sports and USA Today publish a column per format; use the right one.
  3. In superflex, check the quarterback price. The charts give quarterbacks 22 to 35% of all value; CBS Sports sits furthest below the league-aware reference. If you are buying a quarterback, a low chart price helps your pitch.
  4. Expect agreement on order, not on price. The charts rank players much the same; they disagree about exchange rates, especially for tight ends and for the second half of your roster.

How we tested

  • One snapshot: week 9 of 2026. Analyst charts published 2026-11-03 (saved by 2026-11-04); FantasyCalc pulled 2026-11-07 (the weekly-change table uses its 2026-11-03 snapshot). Another week can grade differently.
  • League: 12 teams, our default lineup (QB, 2 RB, 2 WR, TE, FLEX, 7 bench), Half PPR unless noted. Depth is also shown at 10 and 14 teams.
  • Reference: DDF Value, the four redraft charts adjusted for league settings and replacement level and averaged. It measures each chart against the league-aware consensus of the same charts, not against an independent truth. Its position budget, which sets how much value each position gets, averages seven sources' position mixes: the four charts and the three projection sets our model uses as inputs, so three sevenths of that split comes from projections. The projections are not trade charts and are not graded here.
  • Page data: the data file behind this page lists, for the top 150 players by DDF Value, each chart's value as a percent of that chart's #1 player and each player's rank on each chart. The values come from the charts' public pages, saved for this analysis.
  • Short charts: where a chart lists fewer players than a league rosters, DDF Value fills in the missing players from the other charts (for CBS Sports this week, 67 players: the 58 needed to reach the waiver line and a few past it). CBS Sports says an unlisted player is worth up to four points (7.1% of its #1); the fill-in puts its waiver-level running back at 0.6% of its #1.
  • League sizes: every chart is saved at 12 teams; 8, 10 and 14 teams are derived, including for FantasyCalc, which offers other team counts.
  • Data checks: USA Today's week 9 capture carried an amber data-check flag; it is still part of DDF Value.
  • Not tested: age, rookies and injuries (the charts publish no ages, and our injury list is newer than the charts).
  • Grades: the thresholds are ours and written under the scorecard. Methods: statements come from each source's own pages, read 2026-11-07; where a source does not document something, we say so.
